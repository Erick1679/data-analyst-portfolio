Business Intelligence | Analytics Strategy

The Decision Before the Dashboard

Businesses increasingly have access to sophisticated dashboards, automated reports and analytical tools. Yet access to more information does not automatically produce better decisions. The real value of analytics begins with asking the right questions.

By Erick Omondi | October 2026

A business can monitor hundreds of indicators and still struggle to understand what requires attention. Revenue may be growing while profitability declines. Customer registrations may increase while retention weakens. A dashboard can display these patterns, but identifying their significance requires interpretation, context and a clearly defined business objective.

This is why effective analytical work begins before the first chart is created. The analyst must understand the question that the organisation wants answered, the information available and the decisions that may follow.

Start with the problem, not the software

Consider a retailer requesting an analysis of declining sales. A conventional response might compare monthly revenue, calculate growth rates and identify products with weaker performance. These activities are useful, but they are only the beginning.

A more rigorous investigation would examine whether the decline affects particular locations, customer groups or product categories. It would consider changes in pricing, stock availability, returns and seasonal demand. It would also ask whether the apparent decline reflects genuine business conditions or changes in how transactions were recorded.

Each of these questions changes the analytical approach. More importantly, each leads to different potential recommendations.

The purpose of analytics is not simply to describe information. It is to make the information useful for a decision.

Reliable data is the foundation of useful insight

Data quality is frequently misunderstood as a technical concern that can be resolved through routine cleaning. In reality, quality directly influences the credibility of every conclusion.

The United Kingdom Government Data Quality Framework identifies six important dimensions: completeness, uniqueness, consistency, timeliness, validity and accuracy. These dimensions help organisations assess whether available information is suitable for its intended purpose.

For example, a transaction dataset may contain every required field but still include duplicate orders. Customer records may follow the correct format but contain outdated information. A dataset can therefore appear complete without being sufficiently accurate for the decision under consideration.

The analyst must examine these limitations, document significant findings and communicate any uncertainty that could affect the interpretation of the results.

Context gives numbers their meaning

A reported increase in customer complaints may initially suggest worsening service. However, the interpretation could change if the business experienced substantial customer growth during the same period.

The number of complaints matters, but so does the number of customers served, the severity of the issues and whether reporting procedures have changed.

This illustrates why business metrics should be accompanied by relevant definitions, comparisons and explanatory information. Figures become more informative when the reader understands how they were produced and what they represent.

Good analysis ends with a useful recommendation

Analytical reports should connect findings with practical business considerations. Where possible, they should identify which problems require immediate attention, which opportunities warrant investigation and which conclusions remain uncertain.

Recommendations should also reflect the strength of the available evidence. An observed association between two variables does not automatically establish that one causes the other. Further investigation may be necessary before recommending an expensive intervention.

Ultimately, an effective dashboard is not defined by its visual complexity. It is defined by whether it helps the intended audience understand performance, evaluate alternatives and make informed decisions.

Practical takeaway

Before beginning an analytics assignment, establish the decision to be supported, define the necessary measures, assess the quality of the available data and agree on what a useful result should look like.

Predictive Analytics | Machine Learning

Forecasts Are Not Promises

Predictive analytics offers powerful opportunities for planning and decision making. However, an accurate looking prediction is not necessarily a reliable prediction. Understanding uncertainty is essential to using forecasting responsibly.

By Erick Omondi | October 2026

Businesses use forecasts to estimate demand, anticipate revenue, manage inventory and plan operational resources. When these predictions are credible, they support more informed preparation for future conditions.

Problems arise when forecasts are treated as guaranteed outcomes rather than estimates. A model may produce a precise numerical prediction, but that precision does not eliminate uncertainty.

Historical patterns do not guarantee future outcomes

Predictive models learn relationships from historical information. Those relationships may continue into the future, but they may also change because of shifts in consumer behaviour, economic conditions, competition or operational practices.

Consider a retailer using previous sales to estimate future demand. Historical patterns may help identify seasonal increases and recurring customer behaviour. However, unexpected supply disruptions or changes in pricing can reduce the reliability of those predictions.

A credible forecast should therefore be understood as an estimate based on available evidence and stated assumptions.

Model evaluation must respect time

The method used to evaluate a forecasting model is as important as the algorithm. In many forecasting problems, a randomly divided dataset can produce misleading results because information from later periods may influence the training process.

For this reason, chronological evaluation is often more appropriate. The model learns from earlier observations and is tested on later observations that simulate future conditions.

Scikit learn provides a TimeSeriesSplit method designed for sequential data. Its documentation explains why ordinary cross validation may be unsuitable when observations have a meaningful time order.

A model should demonstrate its usefulness on information it could not access during training.

Data leakage can create false confidence

Data leakage occurs when information that would not be available at the time of prediction influences the development or evaluation of a model.

This problem can arise through inappropriate feature construction, incorrect separation of training and testing data or preprocessing procedures that use information from the entire dataset.

A model affected by leakage may appear exceptionally accurate during evaluation but perform poorly when applied to genuinely new observations.

Reliable modelling requires careful separation of training and testing information, appropriate feature engineering and evaluation procedures that reflect how the model will actually be used.

Accuracy should be meaningful to the business

Model performance is often reported through measures such as mean absolute error and root mean squared error. These measures describe different aspects of prediction error, but neither automatically determines whether a forecast is useful.

A prediction error that seems small in one context may have substantial consequences in another.

For an inventory manager, underestimating demand may result in missed sales. Overestimating demand may increase storage costs and unsold stock.

This means that evaluation should consider both mathematical performance and the practical consequences of incorrect predictions.

Transparency strengthens trust

The National Institute of Standards and Technology identifies validity, reliability, transparency and explainability among the important characteristics of trustworthy artificial intelligence.

These principles are relevant to predictive analytics because model users need to understand the assumptions, limitations and potential risks associated with analytical outputs.

A professional forecasting report should explain the prediction period, the available historical information, the evaluation method and the limitations that could influence future performance.

Where uncertainty estimates are available and appropriately validated, presenting a plausible range of outcomes may be more helpful than presenting a single figure. Regular performance reviews are also important when models are used repeatedly.

Practical takeaway

Evaluate forecasts using genuinely unseen periods, compare complex models with reasonable baseline methods, investigate possible data leakage and communicate the limitations of every prediction.

Research Methodology | Professional Writing

Research That Stands Up to Questions

The credibility of professional research depends on more than convincing language. Strong research requires clear questions, appropriate methods, credible evidence and conclusions that can withstand careful examination.

By Erick Omondi | October 2026

Research is valuable when it reduces uncertainty and improves understanding. Whether the assignment involves a business problem, a market investigation or an analytical report, the objective should be to produce findings that are supported by appropriate evidence.

A lengthy report may appear comprehensive, but length alone does not establish quality. A concise report with a sound methodology, reliable sources and a clear argument can be far more useful.

Every strong investigation begins with clarity

Before collecting information, the researcher must establish exactly what the assignment intends to investigate.

Broad topics frequently contain several different research questions. For example, an investigation into digital transformation among small businesses could examine adoption rates, financial barriers, employee skills, operational outcomes or customer expectations.

These subjects are related, but they require different evidence and analytical approaches. Clearly defining the research objective helps determine the appropriate scope, methodology and reporting structure.

Source credibility matters

Research quality depends partly on whether the sources used are appropriate to the claims being made.

Official statistical publications, academic research, institutional reports and documented business records can provide valuable evidence. However, each source should be evaluated according to its purpose, methodology, relevance and limitations.

Information should not be accepted simply because it appears in a professional publication. The researcher should consider who produced the evidence, how it was collected, when it was published and whether alternative explanations exist.

The Office for National Statistics describes statistical quality through important considerations such as relevance, accuracy and reliability, timeliness, accessibility and clarity, and coherence and comparability.

These principles also offer a useful foundation for evaluating evidence in professional research.

Credible research does not simply present an answer. It makes the reasoning behind that answer clear enough to examine.

Methodology connects evidence to conclusions

A professional research report should explain how information was gathered, how relevant material was selected and how findings were interpreted.

In quantitative research, this may involve defining the population, reviewing sampling procedures, describing variables and explaining analytical techniques.

In qualitative research, methodological clarity may require an explanation of participant selection, interview procedures, thematic interpretation and the boundaries of the available evidence.

Neither approach is automatically superior. The appropriate method depends on the research question and the type of evidence needed to answer it.

Interpretation requires intellectual discipline

One of the most important responsibilities of a researcher is distinguishing between what the evidence demonstrates and what the evidence merely suggests.

A statistical relationship between two variables does not necessarily establish that one causes the other. Similarly, the experiences of a small group of respondents cannot automatically represent an entire population.

These limitations do not make research worthless. They define the boundaries within which findings should be interpreted.

Professional integrity requires those boundaries to be communicated clearly.

Writing is part of the analytical process

Research findings have limited value if their intended audience cannot understand them.

Effective analytical writing uses a logical structure, precise language and explanations that connect evidence with conclusions.

An executive summary should communicate the central question, important findings, relevant limitations and practical implications without requiring readers to examine the entire report first.

Charts and tables should clarify the argument rather than merely decorate the document.

Recommendations should reflect the strength of the evidence and remain consistent with the original research objectives.

The value of research lies in its usefulness

A completed research assignment should help its intended audience understand a problem more clearly, evaluate available options or identify questions requiring further investigation.

For a business client, this may involve interpreting market information or investigating operational performance.

For an organisation, it may involve evaluating programme outcomes, identifying information gaps or developing recommendations based on available evidence.

Across these contexts, the underlying expectation remains the same: research should be accurate, transparent, relevant and understandable.

Practical takeaway

Strong research establishes a clear question, uses appropriate evidence, explains its methodology, acknowledges limitations and presents conclusions that follow logically from the findings.
